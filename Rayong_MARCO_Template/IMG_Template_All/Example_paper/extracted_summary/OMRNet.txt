Abstract
Existing Optical Mark Recognition (OMR) systems tend to be expensive and rigid in their
operation, often resulting in erroneous evaluations due to strict correction protocols. This sce-
nario airs the need for a ﬂexible OMR system. Hence, in this work, we propose a lightweight
transfer learning based Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, dubbed as OMRNet,
which can classify answer boxes on any generalized OMR test sheet. Unlike most existing
techniques that rely on image processing algorithms to recognize extracted answer boxes
in two classes: conﬁrmed and empty, the OMRNet is designed to classify the answer boxes
into conﬁrmed, crossed-out, and empty categories. That is, OMRNet is facilitating the cross-
ing out of previously answered questions and thus removing the rigidity of templates in
Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) tests. We have built OMRNet on top of a MobileNetV2
backbone connected to four fully connected layers with appropriate dropouts and activation
functions in between. We have evaluated OMRNet on the Multiple Choice Answer Boxes
dataset available at https://sites.google.com/view/mcq-dataset. We have performed experi-
ments following a 5 fold cross validation scheme, and OMRNet has achieved accuracies of
95.29%, 95.88%, 93.97%, 97.45%, and 97.20%, with an average accuracy of 95.96%. Also,
the experimental results conﬁrm that the present model performs better than the compared
state-of-the-art methods and standard CNN models in terms of accuracy, execution time, and
memory required to store the trained module. Moreover, we have employed a quantization
technique to make the trained module more memory efﬁcient and deployed it to a web app
using our own Representational State Transfer Application Programming Interface (REST
API). It makes OMRNet available via a Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) endpoint, allow-
ing potential users to connect to it via the Internet. The source code for the work is available
at the following link: https://github.com/sa-y-an/OMRNet.

1 Introduction
Multiple Choice Question (MCQ) based assessments are widely used as this evaluation
system is fast and reliable. It removes subjectivity, reduces inter-evaluator variation/bias and
only tests the knowledge of the candidates.
In general, any automated MCQ-based assessment system follows the four steps below:
1. Digitizing the answer sheet - This is usually performed with the help of scanners. With
advancements in digital camera technology and efforts to reduce costs for this operation,
nowadays, answer sheets are being scanned using cameras on webcams and mobiles [20,
31, 48].
2. Candidates’ information and answer boxes extraction - For this, a speciﬁc template,
usually a digital answer sheet, is used. The required information and answer boxes are
usually extracted using image registration methods.
3. Acquiring candidate information - This process is used to recognize candidates’ iden-
tiﬁcation information, which is beyond the scope of this work. However, it is performed
by either detecting a particular pattern on the answer sheet used in the OMR system as
described in [20] or using advanced Optical Character Recognition (OCR) techniques as
demonstrated by Chai [11].
4. Recognition of responses - Usually, for grading, an answer box is classiﬁed into two
categories:markedandunmarked(empty).Therefore,simpleimageprocessingtechniques
can be applied to segregate a box based on a speciﬁc threshold of ink present on the box.
A vastly empty box would refer to an unmarked answer, whereas a vastly ﬁlled box would
qualify as a marked box.
Step 4 among the above steps limits the ﬂexibility of traditional MCQ based assessment
processes as it doesn’t provide any scope for crossing out an erroneously marked answer.
Figure 1 illustrates the problem in greater detail. Furthermore, any unintentional mark or
scratch on the answer sheet (see Fig. 1) may cause standard OMR systems to misrecognize
answers. Furthermore, a small misalignment of the scanner with respect to the OMR sheet
adversely affects the ability of algorithms to classify answer boxes [5, 50].

Generally, answer sheet templates vary depending on the assessment systems. This forces
both the candidate and the evaluating team to follow a speciﬁc set of instructions to smoothly
prepare and utilize the answer sheet template used in the OMR system. Consequently, mis-
prints and unplanned deviations from the reference sheet often lead to erroneous evaluations.
To overcome these constraints, we need a ﬂexible OMR system that provides an opportu-
nity to cancel out a previously marked option and accommodate small misalignments. Thus,
this type of OMR system needs to classify an answer box into three classes: conﬁrmed,
crossed out, and empty. A sample answer sheet containing such markings is shown in Fig. 2.
A brief overview of the 3 types of answer boxes that might appear in an answer sheet
supported by the ﬂexible OMR system mentioned above is described below, while some
sample answer box images are shown in Fig. 4.
– Conﬁrmed - The answer boxes that are chosen by the candidates as the correct answer.
The correct answer can be marked by ﬁlling the answer box (see Fig. 3) or by putting a
tick mark on a answer (see Fig. 2).

Thus in deep learning research in modern days, we can ﬁnd signiﬁcant developments for
making a lightweight model, as can be found in the works [6, 45].
In light of the above discussions, in this work, we have tried to come up with a lightweight
neural network architecture that can easily classify test answer box images into any one of
the three classes: conﬁrmed, crossed out, and empty. We have named our model OMRNet.
It consists of a MobileNet V2 [49] backbone followed by 4 fully connected layers each
having a dropout of 0.25 and a ﬁnal softmax layer. In this work, instead of training a model
from scratch, we have used transfer learning, which requires fewer training samples to train
a model properly. OMRNet thus uses a 4-layer fully connected (FC) network on top of
the image feature maps obtained from pre-trained MobileNetV2 (trained on the ImageNet
dataset). Furthermore, we have used a post-training quantization technique to quantize the
proposed model and reduce its size before deployment.
The major contributions of our work are summarized below -
– We have designed a lightweight architecture that can classify an answer box into one of
three classes: conﬁrmed, crossed out, and empty.
– We have used the concept of transfer learning as it helps train a model with fewer training
samples.
– Two relatively new non-monotonic activation functions: Power Function Linear Unit
(PFLU) and Faster PFLU (FPFLU), introduced by Zhu et al. [63], have been used in
conjunction with Rectiﬁed Linear Unit (ReLU) [3] in the model layers.
– The entire architecture is evaluated on a publicly available dataset. Experimental results
conﬁrm the superiority of OMRNet over state-of-the-art methods.
– We have deployed our proposed classiﬁer to a web app using a Representational State
Transfer Application Programming Interface (REST API), which on uploading an answer
box, returns its predicted class and conﬁdence score.
– We have used a quantization algorithm to reduce the size of the trained model so that it
can be used on edge devices and webapps through REST APIs. The new size is 7 times
lesser as compared to the non-quantized model with a 0.40% drop in average accuracy
on the present test set.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the literature survey
of the major works done in this ﬁeld. Section 3 elaborates on our proposed work, where
we have tried to address the justiﬁcation of different steps used here. Section 4 includes
a description of the dataset in use, testing and training protocols, performance analysis in
terms of execution time, model size, recognition accuracy, comparison with state-of-the-art
techniques and CNN models, etc. In Section 5, we conclude the paper and discuss the possible
future extensions of this work.
2 Literature survey
In the past, OMR devices, in general used costly and bulky image scanners (hardware) for
grading responses to any MCQ tests. Here we have discussed a few of the works that are most
relevant to our work. However, readers can gain a better understanding of the problem by
reading the review work by Elias et al. [19], in which the authors discuss the advancements
and development of OMR technologies in greater detail.
Hussman and Deng [26] developed a fast OMR hardware that was capable of dealing with
three different shapes of MC answer boxes: circle, oval, and rectangle. Some of the early
OMR systems used a histogram of binary image pixels to recognize the MC answer boxes

[14]. However, with the increasing popularity of MCQ based assessments, efforts to replace
image scanners with edge devices like mobile phones, cameras, and webcams have started
gaining popularity. Some of the notable methods in this ﬁeld were proposed by Nguyen et
al. [46], Rasiq et al. [48] and China et al. [13]. Nguyen et al. [46] presented a low cost
system based on a digital camera instead of scanners for image acquisition and answer box
extraction. They ﬁrst employed skew adjustment and normalization steps to locate the answer
boxes in an answer sheet used for OMR systems, and then with the help of black pixel count,
they localized the answer boxes. An application for mobile based MCQ test analysis and
evaluation was proposed by Rasiq et al. [48]. In this work, the researchers ﬁrst scanned the
MCQ answer sheet using mobile and then the answer region was cropped manually. Finally,
using black pixel count, the conﬁrmed responses were identiﬁed from the cropped region.
An android application for the recognition and grading of multiple choice answer sheets was
proposed by both China et al. [13] and Krisnadi et al. [36]. All these methods did not provide
any option to cancel out an erroneously marked response on the answer sheet.
Other than not providing an option to cross out an answer, another major challenge that
modern-day OMR systems face is the extraction of skewed region of interests (ROI) due
to misalignment of scanners. Sanguanst et al. [50] showed how misalignment adversely
affects the recognition success of software based algorithms. AL-Marakeby [5] showed that
the majority of errors caused by modern-day thresholding algorithms are a consequence of
failure to detect the marks. For OMR systems utilizing algorithms that use a reference sheet
for evaluation [48], misalignment skews the results by a huge margin. To tackle this issue
Chai [11] came up with a solution using a solid black mark around answer boxes. But using
such a method makes a system rigid on the template used.
Another major reason for the inaccuracy of modern day OMR systems is that users are
often unfamiliar with the OMR evaluation technique speciﬁc to the template they are using.
Weaver and Chalkley [61] showed that having an expert for explaining to the users about the
marking procedure is essential for good accuracy. However, this creates an extra overhead.
Further, Khan et al. [32] showed that having rules to ﬁll an OMR sheet leads to time waste
and wrong marking.
Apart from these, some researchers opted for designing a relatively ﬂexible OMR system
where a candidate can cancel out some marked responses in the answer sheet of the underlying
OMR system. Spadaccini et al. [56] introduced a new term known as squareness (deﬁned as
the absolute difference between the number of black pixels in the columns and rows), which
helped in distinguishing crossed out answer boxes from the conﬁrmed cases. Fisteus et al. [20]
proposed a webcam system known as “Eyegrade", which ﬁrst used a two-stage thresholding
process to identify answer boxes and then followed a similar approach as described in [56]
to distinguish crossed out and conﬁrm answer boxes in the answer sheet.
Some of the recent methods have applied different image processing algorithms to improve
the precision of OMR answer matching [29] in real time [28]. However, they only consider
empty and conﬁrmed cases. Sinchai et al. recently suggested an arithmetic sequence to esti-
mate undetected existing circle choice locations, thereby improving the precision of various
OMR schemes [54]. Image processing techniques have also been used to retrieve information
from damaged OMR sheets [4].
Generally, a detector extracts the answer boxes from an answer sheet following one of the
two approaches described below [30].
– Pixel Based - In this approach, the detector tries to identify answer boxes along with the
class information by studying the pixel signatures of the image.

– Object Based - In this approach, the detector identiﬁes answer boxes along with class
information using global features formed by the coalition of the pixels (e.g., shapes on
any images).
Both of the aforementioned approaches can be either supervised or unsupervised [30].
In the case of supervised learning, the classiﬁers try to learn the features from the answer
box image samples, where it is considered that the pixels are already mapped to form the
answer box images. Whereas, in unsupervised learning, the classiﬁers aim at grouping the
pixels based on similarities and try to group them into different classes [30]. Based on these
approaches, Gyamﬁand Missah [22] came up with a pixel based unsupervised classiﬁcation
approach, wherein they proposed an OMR system to detect shaded options of candidates in
MCQ type examinations. Their classiﬁcation was based on the pixels making up the image,
along with other image preprocessing strategies.
As evident from the above discussion, most of the work done on OMR is related to the
extraction and recognition of answer boxes from answer sheets used in the OMR system.
The application of CNN to classify answer boxes is fairly new and highly unexplored. This
may be due to the unavailability of the voluminous data required for CNN model training.
However, a notable piece of work was carried out recently by Aﬁﬁand Hussain [1], where
an ML based technique was introduced to increase the ﬂexibility of the MCQ based test
assessment process. Their major contribution was to introduce a dataset of answer boxes and
to design a classiﬁer based on their database. The proposed work is an improvement on the
work proposed by Aﬁﬁand Hussain [1].
Apart from this, there are some works related to recognizing handwritten MCQ exams
(where a candidate’s response is either ‘a’, ‘b’, ‘c’ or any other character of the English
alphabet). Shaikh et al. [52] proposed a system that used a personal computer, a portable
scanner, and an application program that automatically corrected the handwritten answer
sheets using a CNN model. Supic et al. proposed a rather simple and effective algorithm that
enabled the automated reading of a limited set of handwritten answers and minimized the
need for human intervention in the scanning process [57].
Some methods have also been developed to automate the process of OMR/OCR and MCQ
test grading using artiﬁcial vision [9], auto resonance networks [47] and character recognition
techniques [35]. Mobile and web-based applications for the evaluation of MCQ tests have
also gained interest in recent years, as seen from the research by Calado et al. [10] and Rasiq
et al. [48]. However, after extracting the Region of Interest (ROI) they use thresholding based
algorithms to recognize the answer mark.
From the above discussion, we observe that Aﬁﬁand Hussain [1] proposed a ﬂexible OMR
system where the candidates have the option to nullify his/her responses by crossing them
out. However, they suggested a two-stage CNN based method, which is computationally
intensive. Also, the performance of their work is not satisfactory. Keeping these issues in
mind, in this paper, our primary objective is to explore a CNN based model to improve the
existing solution in terms of computation and storage requirements, and to make it compatible
with edge devices. There are some associated challenges with the existing methods that we
have tried to address in the proposed work. The challenges and our solutions are listed
below:
– In general, methods following a two-stage or two-pass approach (e.g., [40, 41]) are
computationally expensive compared to a single pass method [7]. Thus for edge devices,
two-stage methods might not be suitable. Hence, we have tried to ﬁnd a solution that
follows a single pass approach..

3 Proposed work
With the exponential increase in computing power and access to abundant data, neural net-
works (deep neural networks in particular) have certainly revolutionized the ﬁeld of Artiﬁcial
Intelligence. Hence, many complex tasks, like image recognition and segmentation across
various domains, can be performed with impressive accuracy. However, this excellent accu-
racy of state-of-the-art models often comes at the cost of high computing power, which is
far beyond the capabilities of most modern mobile devices. The problem we have tried to
address (i.e., the classiﬁcation of answer boxes in an OMR sheet) will ﬁnd its major appli-
cation in resource constrained environments. To be speciﬁc, in this work, we have proposed
a lightweight model while signiﬁcantly improving its accuracy on the diverse MC Answer
Boxes dataset Section 4.1.
3.1 Architecture of OMRNet
Since one of the primary goals of our work is to build a lightweight OMR system, we have
prepared the OMRNet on top of the MobileNetV2 model speciﬁcally designed for resource
constrained environments by Sandler et al. [49]. Furthermore, because CNN-based models
in general require a lot of data, we have used the concept of transfer learning in OMRNet
instead of building and training the model from scratch. Thus, OMRNet includes features of
MobileNetV2 (pre-trained on ImageNet) with a deep multiplier of 1.0 and an input image
size of 224 × 224 pixels2.
The OMRNet architecture consists of 3 dense FC layers with activation functions: RELU,
PFLU, and FPFLU on the top of the convolutional part of the MobileNetV2. At the end
of each layer, a dropout of 25% of the total number of parameters is added to prevent the
overﬁtting of the model during the training process. The ﬁnal FC layer is followed by a
3-class classiﬁcation layer with Softmax as the activation function. The entire architecture is
shown in Fig. 5.

For our purpose of answer box classiﬁcation, we make use of the feature maps of the

duced by Sander et al. [49] in 2018 with the sole purpose of improving the performance of
lightweight mobile models on multiple tasks and benchmarks. This novel neural network
architecture is speciﬁcally tailored for mobile and resource-constrained environments and
delivers high accuracy while signiﬁcantly reducing the memory footprint.
It achieves this optimal balance in performance and accuracy by keeping the parameters
and mathematical operations as low as possible by using depth wise convolution. Moreover,
to prevent the destruction of too much information due to nonlinearities, it introduces a
new layer module: the inverted residual with a linear bottleneck. This layer encapsulates the
inner layers in a bottleneck residual input4 as shown in Fig. 5. Intuitively, it can be thought
that the intermediate inputs and outputs are encoded by these linear bottleneck layers of
MobileNetV2. Furthermore, it has shortcut connections between these bottlenecks to enable
faster training and better accuracy. Since the major application of this answer box classiﬁer
would be on low-cost devices, we chose the image feature vectors of MobileNetV2 as our
transfer learning model.
3.3 Activation functions
An activation function is arguably one of the most important units in a deep neural network. It
is what sets a neural network apart from a logistic regression model. A neural network without
an activation function would just be constrained to calculate and optimize linear functions.
Traditionally, sigmoid and tanh functions are used as activation functions in the literature of
deep learning. However, once the neurons get saturated, it tends to kill the gradient (as at
high values, both the sigmoid and tanh functions approach zero). Since the back-propagation
depends highly on the gradient this blocks the information ﬂow through the neurons to their
weights and consequently to their data5. To solve this issue, the ReLU activation function
was introduced in [3]. In most cases, ReLU does a good job as an activation function and
provides faster convergence. However, due to their fragile nature, ReLU units die irreversibly
during the training process. ReLU units can get knocked off the data manifold.
To overcome this problem, in the subsequent layers, we have used two newly developed
non-monotonic activation functions, namely PFLU and FPFLU, introduced by Zhu et al. [63].
The negative parts of these activation functions are non-monotonic (see Fig. 6). Therefore,
these activation functions maintain the sparsity of the negative part while introducing negative
activation values and nonzero derivative values for the negative part. This leads to better
training than ReLU. The shapes of said two activation functions are shown in Fig. 6. Details
of these activation functions are provided below.

3.4 Fully connected layers
FC layers are deﬁned as the layers where all the inputs from one layer are connected to every
activation unit of the next layer. FC layers are generally used at the end of any neural network
architecture for detecting speciﬁc global conﬁgurations of the features detected by the lower
layers in the neural network (in our case, the image feature vectors of MobileNetV2). They
are good at extracting high level features speciﬁc to a dataset. Thus, we have used 3 FC
layers to extract the global features relevant to our answer box classiﬁcation problem based
on the lower level features obtained from the feature maps of the MobileNetV2 model trained
on ImageNet. The FC layers follow a converging structure based on the number of features
available in the MobileNetV2 feature vectors (which is 1240). Thus, the 3 FC layers have a
size of 640, 320, and 160 respectively.
3.5 Dropout
Neural networks sometimes learn statistical noise as a feature of the dataset. Although this
can improve accuracy in the training data, it can adversely affect test accuracy. To minimize
the effect of this overﬁtting, we have randomly discarded 25% of neurons from each FC
layer.
4 Experimental results and analysis
This section explains and analyses the experimental results obtained using the proposed
OMRNet for classifying the answer boxes present in the OMR sheets. All the experiments
are carried out on the free-to-use Kaggle kernel. Kaggle Kernel is a free platform that lets us

(version 2.4.1) to implement our model. In this section, we have described the dataset in use,
the experimental setup, the OMRNet training process, and model performances by varying
the different units of the OMRNet.

4.1 Dataset description
For experimental purposes, we have used the 5-fold cross-validation sets for the classiﬁcation
of the samples provided under MC Answer Box dataset6. The dataset is pre-organized for 5-
fold cross validation experiments by Aﬁﬁand Hussain [1]. The dataset consists of rectangular
shaped MC answer box images divided uniformly into three classes namely, conﬁrmed,
crossed out, and empty. The conﬁrmed class consists of the answer box images which the
candidates had chosen to be the correct answer, the crossed out class comprises the answer
box images that were canceled by the candidates, and the empty class includes the blank
answer box images.
The major reasons for choosing this dataset for our experiments are :
– There is no uniformity in the shape and size of the answer boxes. Furthermore, the images
have varying light intensities. This helped us simulate the situations where the answer
sheet is placed on the scanner in a skewed manner.
– This has helped us simulate situations where the scanner is skewed with respect to the
answer sheet.
– The dataset contains answer sheets following various templates. This has assisted us in
making our model template agnostic.
– The answer marking pattern also varies from one sheet to another. This has helped our
model accommodate the varying ﬁll patterns of various sheets.
Overall, the dataset is quite diverse, which has helped our model accommodate diverse
features.
The entire dataset consists of 10200 MC Answer Box images, divided into 5 folds. Each
fold contains 2040 images (680 samples per class). It is to be mentioned that to increase
the number of crossed out images and balance the number of images in all 3 classes, three
types of image augmentation: translation, rotation, and ﬂipping (both vertical and horizontal)
were performed by the researchers who made this dataset public. Some sample images of
the dataset from fold 1 are shown in Fig. 4.
4.2 Data preprocessing
It is well understood that any ML model is only as good as the data it receives. Hence, we
performed some preprocessing on the input answer box images prior to feeding the same to
the OMRNet model. At the outset, every answer box image is resized to a ﬁxed dimension of
224 × 224 × 3, similar to the standard image dimension that a MobileNetV2 model takes as
input. For resizing the images, we have used bi-linear interpolation, wherein a new pixel value
is interpolated from a linear combination of four of the nearest pixel values [62]. Further,
we have normalized each image pixel values within the range [0, 1] by dividing each pixel
intensity by 255 since deep neural networks tend to work better with smaller inputs.

4.6 Performance analysis in terms of execution time and network size
One of the best features of our model is its lightweightness. We have been able to strike
an optimum balance between accuracy and performance in terms of memory efﬁciency.
Even though our model does signiﬁcantly better than other state-of-the-art architectures (as
discussed in the previous sections), it does not come at the cost of a high processing power
requirement. This allows it to be a perfect ﬁt for deploying on mobile and edge devices and
other resource constrained environments. Two of the most important parameters for a model
to be lightweight are its speed and size. Thus in the following subsections, we have compared
our model’s speed and size with state-of-the-art models.
4.6.1 Training and evaluation time comparison
Speed is a crucial ingredient for any model that has real life applications. A faster model can
lead to the faster calculation of results and is often the need of the hour. Furthermore, a faster
model generally implies less computing power being drawn.
In the following subsections, we have ﬁrst compared our model’s performance by varying
feature vectors with state-of-the-art models’ feature vectors ( all of which are trained on
ImageNet ). Next, we have compared our model with the performance of various state-of-
the-art models trained on the dataset we have worked on.
All these performances are evaluated on Kaggle kernels as described at begining of Section
4. For the purpose of comparing the speed, we have used two parameters namely -
1. Total training time - This refers to the total time the model takes to train on the ﬁve
folds. A model that takes higher time to train would be undesirable.
2. Evaluation time - This refers to the the time the trained model takes to classify all the
images in the ﬁve folds. A heavier architecture would take a longer time to classify an
image. Since an image can come in any shape or size, we use the time it takes to classify
all the images of the dataset to get a good estimate.
OMRNet takes 8 minutes and 28 seconds to train and it takes 22.4 seconds to classify all
the answer boxes of the dataset. This implies on average it can classify 455.35 answer boxes
per second (on the Kaggle kernel).
Performance comparison by using feature vectors of other models: We have used the fea-
ture vectors of MobileNetV2 [49] for building OMRNet. It has given both better performance
and accuracy compared to other popular architectures (DenseNet-201, EfﬁcientNet-B0,
VGG-19, and ResNet-50). EfﬁcientNet-B0’s feature vectors had the second fastest train-
ing times, i.e., 10 minutes 22 seconds. Using the feature vectors of VGG-19 made the model
the slowest, and it took 65 times more time for evaluation and 119 times more time for
training. This means that on average it can classify a mere 7.07 answer boxes per second.

Existing systems that can extract the answer boxes using orthodox ROI extraction methods
(as discussed in Section 2) can send a post request to our API to know its class (conﬁrmed,
crossed out, or empty). Once the class of an answer box is known, they can score the answer
sheet accordingly. A snapshot of the REST API of our model is shown in Fig. 13.
One of the main aims of developing a REST API for OMRNet is to facilitate its deployment
to edge devices like smartphones. To tackle the problem of limited computational resources
and storage in such devices we have used model quantization to improve the lightweight
nature of our model, thus making its deployment faster and less storage intensive. We have
performed post-training for our model using the TensorFlow Lite and TensorFlow Model
Optimization libraries. Ultimately, the ﬁnal model size before deployment through the REST
API is 3 MB with a drop in accuracy of 0.40% on average.
4.7.1 Model quantization
Model quantization is a technique used to reduce the memory and computational require-
ments of deep learning models. It is typically performed by reducing the number of bits
used to represent the weights and activation of a model. We have used post-training model
quantization with the following steps.
– Training: The entire precision model has been trained on the present dataset as usual.
– Quantization: The weights and activations of the trained model have been quantized to a
lower-bit representation. We have used the algorithm proposed by [27] for this purpose.
– Retraining: The quantized model has been retrained on the dataset to ﬁne-tune the quan-
tized weights and activation. This step has been performed to compensate for the loss of
accuracy caused by quantization.
– Optimization: The quantized model has been optimized for deployment by pruning unim-
portant weights and removing unnecessary layers.
We have used the quantization algorithm proposed by Jacob et al. [27] which uses integer
arithmetic to approximate the ﬂoating-point computations in a neural network. This helped
us reduce the trained model size 7 times. From 21 MB we were able to reduce the model size
to 3 MB. The results of the model quantization are summarized in Fig. 14. In this ﬁgure, the
ﬁrst ﬁve scores represent the performances of each fold of ﬁve-fold experiments, while the
last column represents the average test accuracy. As evident from the ﬁgure, post quantization
the accuracy drops by 0.4% on average.

5 Conclusion and future work
The existing OMR techniques, in general, use hard-coded image processing algorithms.
This hampers the ﬂexibility of the system and also demands extra caution while marking
and handling the sheet. A few systems exist that rely on deep neural networks to classify
answer boxes but they are not enough lightweight to be suitable for resource constraint
environments. These combined with the need to remove the rigidity in the correction of MCQ
tests motivated us to develop our proposed model. To this end, we propose a lightweight CNN
model, called OMRNet, which performs reasonably well in classifying answer boxes without
compromising on performance.
OMRNet provides high accuracy (95.96 %) on a complex and generalized dataset and
is seen to be the best performing model of its class in the domain of ﬂexible generalized
OMR techniques according to the best of our knowledge. However, it inherits some of the
deﬁciencies of a CNN classiﬁer and performs poorly in distinguishing between classes with
a large number of similar features. Future work would include building a complete mobile
app, where on insertion of an OMR sheet with its answer key, the system gives back the score
of the test giver and its use in practice. This will lead to the collection of more real-world
data and hence diversify the original dataset, allowing us to use the concepts of data-driven
development and MLOps [38]. Consequently, training and tuning our model on that diverse
dataset would lead to better predictive accuracy. In a problem such as the one our paper
tries to solve, there is no room for error. A single wrongly classiﬁed answer box can be the
difference between a student passing, and failing. It can make or break a career, especially in
